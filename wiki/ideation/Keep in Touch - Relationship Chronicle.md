> [!IMPORTANT] Key Takeaway
> **Why this matters:** A professional relationship runs across email, KakaoTalk, WhatsApp, LinkedIn, and calls — so **the thread doesn't exist anywhere**. Before writing to a mentor or a contact I scroll back through the wrong app trying to remember what we said, whether I already told them this, whether I'm about to repeat a mistake. The friction isn't intent; it's reconstruction.
> **How to use it:** Give every contact **one continuous thread that sits above the channels** — a dated log of what was said wherever it was said — plus a cadence, so career updates to mentors, 명절 인사, and follow-ups go out on time and land specific.
> **Informs:** A career-network instrument: the maintained network as a professional asset (mentors, advisors, former colleagues, recruiters, HR pipelines).

# Keep in Touch — Relationship Chronicle

## Problem Statement — the Thread Doesn't Exist Anywhere
**This is the core.** A single professional relationship runs across email, KakaoTalk, WhatsApp, LinkedIn DMs, and phone calls that leave no record at all. No app holds the relationship; each one holds a *fragment* of it. So the continuous conversation — the thing that actually is the relationship — exists nowhere except my memory, which is the least reliable place it could be.

What that produces, every time I go to reach out:

1. **The scroll-back tax.** Before writing, I open the app — usually the wrong one first — and scroll up through months of messages trying to reconstruct where we left off. The cost of *resuming* is high enough that I sometimes just don't.
2. **"Did I already tell them this?"** The specific anxiety that kills the message. I can't remember whether I already shared the update, already asked about the role, already sent the link. So I either repeat myself or say nothing.
3. **"Am I about to make the same mistake again?"** Last time something landed badly, or they mentioned something sensitive, or I promised to follow up and didn't. Without a record I'm guessing, and the guess makes me hesitant.
4. **The timing is invisible.** Nothing signals that a relationship has gone cold. By the time I notice, reaching out feels awkward — *"why now, after this long?"*

The result: the people most worth knowing quietly flatten into contact-list entries — not from indifference, but because resuming costs too much.

## Core Product Thesis — One Thread Above the Channels
**The chronicle is the product.** Not a reminder app with notes attached — a channel-agnostic conversation thread, one per person, that the reminders happen to sit on top of.

1. **The chronicle (the core).** Every contact leaves a dated entry regardless of where it happened — email, KakaoTalk, WhatsApp, a call, a coffee. Read down a person's page and you see **the continuous flow of the relationship**, in one place, in order. No messaging app can give you this, because each one only ever holds its own fragment.
2. **The pre-flight read.** The moment of value is the ten seconds *before* writing: last contact, what we discussed, what's still open, what I owe them, what to avoid. That answers "did I already tell them this?" and "am I repeating a mistake?" — the two questions that currently stop the message from being sent. Zero scrolling.
3. **The list** — who I need to stay close to, maintained deliberately rather than inferred from whoever happened to message me last.
4. **The cadence** — each contact carries their own rhythm (monthly / quarterly / yearly / 명절만). The system watches the clock so I don't have to.

Order matters. Reminders without the thread produce hollow check-ins ("hey, long time!"). The thread without reminders is a diary nobody opens. But the thread is the hard part and the defensible part — the reminder is a feature any calendar has.

**The one-line version:** every messaging app organizes by *channel*; this organizes by *person*, and that's the whole idea.

## Who It's For — the Career Network
*(The angle the [source](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=M-ZREjfmqe8&list=PLB7PYmHaa-5p_vJ3sUf3TuDiKn3zUJvNp) actually uses it for, and the primary framing of this idea.)*

He runs the list as a **career instrument**: a set of people he wants advice or mentorship from, and a habit of sending them his **career update on a regular cadence** — not a request, just "here's where I am now." Three things fall out of that:

- **The update *is* the reason to write.** It removes the awkward-opening problem the way 명절 does culturally. You're not asking for anything, so nothing needs justifying — and when you eventually do need advice, the relationship is already warm.
- **Staying visible beats staying in contact.** Mentors and advisors forget you not because they stopped caring but because you stopped appearing. A periodic update keeps you in the room.
- **In HR, the list *is* the resource.** For anyone in or around hiring, the maintained network — who they are, what they're doing now, when we last spoke, what they were looking for — is the core professional asset. Most people hold it in memory and LinkedIn, which means they hold a fraction of it.

**Adjacent variant — cold mail & follow-up.** The same primitives cover outbound: recruiters, hiring managers, founders. Sent → +1 week nudge → +3 weeks final → close out, with a log of what I pitched, what they answered, which angle got a reply. Warm and cold are one continuum — a cold contact who replies becomes a relationship to maintain. Two tools today; one list here.

## The Korean Layer — 명절 인사
In Korea, holiday 안부 is not optional politeness — and it is at least as professional as it is personal. 선배, 전 직장 상사, 거래처, 멘토: 설날 and 추석 (plus 연말, 승진, 이직) are the sanctioned moments when reaching out needs **no excuse**. The calendar supplies the reason.

That makes holidays a natural anchor:
- **The list gets one scheduled sweep per 명절** rather than a trickle of individual nudges.
- **Tiers matter** — who gets a call, who gets a written message, who gets a line. The list should hold that distinction.
- **The log prevents the copy-paste tell** — with each person's chronicle open, the greeting references their actual year instead of being an obviously mass-sent 새해 복 많이 받으세요.
- **The failure it fixes is specific:** every 명절 I remember a few people *after* the window closes. A maintained list makes the omission a decision, not an accident.

## 💭 Reflection: The Calendar Already Hands You the Excuse
The hardest part of staying in touch isn't intent — it's the awkwardness of the opening. Write to someone after a year of silence and the first line has to justify itself. Write to a mentor and it's worse: silence, then a message, reads as *I need something*.

명절 removes that. Everyone is messaging everyone, so nobody asks why you're writing. Korea has, in effect, institutionalized the solution to relationship drift: twice a year the culture forces a full sweep of everyone who matters to you. I've never treated it as infrastructure, but that's exactly what it is.

The career update is the same mechanism, self-issued. If I send where-I-am-now on a rhythm, I never have to manufacture a reason again — and the ask, when it comes, arrives on a warm line instead of a cold one.

What I don't have is the other half. The occasion comes back on schedule; my memory doesn't. Every 명절 I work from whoever happens to surface in recent messages, which means the people I've drifted furthest from — the ones the ritual is *for* — are precisely the ones I miss. A maintained list inverts that: the longest-silent contact rises to the top instead of falling off the bottom.

## Core User Flow
1. **Add a person** — name, how we know each other, why they matter, desired cadence.
2. **Log a contact** — after a call, an email, a KakaoTalk exchange: a few lines on what came up, tagged with the channel. Dated automatically. *(The channel is metadata; the thread is the point.)*
3. **Get nudged** — when someone crosses their cadence, they surface. Around 명절 the whole list surfaces at once.
4. **Read before writing** — open the person and see the thread in order: last contact and where, what we discussed, what's still open, what I owe them, what not to repeat. This replaces scrolling back through three apps.
5. **Reach out** — with actual context, so the message is specific instead of generic.
6. **Or broadcast** — send one update (career news, 명절 인사) to a *segment* of the list, personalized per person from their chronicle. One-to-many without reading as mass-sent.
7. **Review** — periodically prune and re-rank; relationships change tier, and that's fine.

## 📡 Landscape (verified 2026-07-31)
*(Web scan, not product trials — pricing and feature claims are vendor/review-sourced.)*

**The closest thing already exists: [Clay](https://clay.earth/use-case/imessage-crm) (now rebranded Mesh).** It markets itself as "the CRM for iMessage" and does the exact cross-channel thesis — auto-creates contact cards from people you've texted and integrates email, calendar, iMessage, WhatsApp, LinkedIn, Twitter/Facebook into one card, surfacing when you last spoke. This is the uncomfortable finding: the core idea is built, on Apple's stack, for the Western messaging world. Notably, it requires **full disk access** to read iMessage, which reviewers flag as a trust problem.

| Layer | Who's there | What they solve |
|:--|:--|:--|
| Cross-channel personal CRM | **Clay/Mesh**, [Dex](https://getdex.com/blog/personal-crm-for-networking/) (LinkedIn + Gmail + contacts sync, keep-in-touch reminders), Covve (~$9.99/mo, "when you're losing touch"), Monica, UpHabit | Contact cards + auto-capture + cadence reminders. Dex/Clay/folk are the consensus 2026 picks for *networking* specifically |
| Team / sales CRM | HubSpot, folk, Attio, NetHunt, Connectoo | Unified contact timeline across WhatsApp/SMS/email/VoIP — solved thoroughly, but priced and framed for revenue teams |
| Recruiting CRM (the HR case) | [Gem](https://www.greenhouse.com/blog/best-recruitment-automation-software), Beamery, Phenom, Avature, iCIMS — enterprise, **$10K–35K+/yr** | Nurturing passive candidates over long cycles. Gem's 2026 benchmark: **46% of sourced hires in 2025 came from people already in the CRM/ATS**, up from 26% five years earlier — the "maintained network is the asset" thesis, quantified |
| Mentoring platforms | Qooper, Mentorloop, MentorcliQ | Check-in scheduling and nudges — but built for the **program administrator** running a company mentorship program, not for the individual maintaining their own mentors |
| Default behavior | LinkedIn post, memory, a Notion table | Public, algorithmic, or frictional |

**Where the gaps actually are** — four, in rough order of how defensible they look:

1. **Korea is empty.** A Korean-language search surfaced no individual 인맥 chronicle product — only Kakao Bizmessage / Bizgo (B2B bulk messaging) and old contact-book apps. Clay's whole capture strategy is iMessage + WhatsApp, which is close to irrelevant in a KakaoTalk market. The strongest incumbent is structurally mismatched to the market you'd start in.
2. **The individual side of the mentoring/HR relationship.** Every mentoring platform serves the org; every recruiting CRM serves the hiring team at enterprise price. Nobody serves *the person* maintaining their own advisors, or the individual recruiter whose network outlives their employer.
3. **The private targeted update.** A career update to twenty named people is not a LinkedIn post (public, algorithmic, seen by the wrong crowd) and not a sales sequence (built to convert strangers). Nobody productizes it.
4. **Pre-flight brief as the primary surface.** Existing tools are organized around contact cards and reminder queues. "Read this before you write" as the main screen is a positioning nobody occupies — though it's a feature, not a moat.

## ⚠️ The Honest Framing — This *Is* CRM, Pointed at Yourself
No point pretending otherwise. Contacts, last-touch date, interaction log, cadence, segments, sequenced follow-up, personalized broadcast — every primitive here maps 1:1 onto what sales CRM has sold for twenty years. The only substitution is the account: a company's revenue pipeline becomes my career.

Which reframes the central question. Not *"is this a good idea?"* but:

> **Why is CRM-for-revenue an industry while CRM-for-yourself has been attempted repeatedly and never broken out?**

It isn't product quality — Monica, Dex, Clay/Mesh, Covve, UpHabit are all competent. The difference is structural:

| | Sales CRM | Personal / career CRM |
|:--|:--|:--|
| Who logs | A rep whose **employer mandates it**, whose comp depends on the pipeline | Me, unpaid, on my own time |
| What keeps it alive | A manager reviews it; it is literally the job | Nothing — so it decays in weeks |
| Payoff | This quarter, measurable, attributable | Vague, years out, unattributable |
| Who pays | The company, $10K+/yr without blinking | Me, reluctantly, ~$10/mo |

**Salespeople don't log because CRM is delightful. They log because someone makes them.** Remove the employer and the entire mechanism sustaining the data disappears. That — not UX, not features — is why this category keeps stalling, and it means "can logging be made near-free?" isn't a nice-to-have but the actual business problem.

Only three substitutes for the boss look viable:

1. **Automate the logging away entirely** — Clay/Mesh's bet, which is why it asks for full disk access, and why that ask is its weak point.
2. **Put an employer back in** — sell to individual recruiters and agency sourcers, where the network *is* the job and budget exists. Gem's 46%-of-sourced-hires number says someone is already being paid for exactly this.
3. **Make the output the reason to log** — I don't maintain the record for its own sake; I maintain it because it *writes my quarterly career update*. The log becomes a byproduct of something I wanted anyway. Needs neither invasive access nor a boss.

## 🎯 The Way Out — Don't Build CRM, Build It for One Market
*(From [Pigment's Eléonore Crespo, "How to Steal Customers From Giants 600x Bigger than Your Startup"](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=5ehxutchdXU) — see [[../research/inspiration|Inspiration]].)*

The category she entered already existed — planning, owned by spreadsheets and by Anaplan. She didn't win with a better general planning tool; she rebuilt it **customised around one function's specific purpose: finance**. The giant stays general and therefore shallow; the challenger goes narrow and therefore deep.

That resolves the "isn't this just CRM?" problem above. **The answer is not to build a better horizontal CRM — it's to pick one market and build the relationship tool that market actually needs.** The failure mode of every personal CRM is that it stayed horizontal: a generic contact-and-reminder app for "everyone who has relationships," which means nobody's specific workflow, nobody's specific payoff, and no reason anyone keeps it fed.

**Proof the verticalization works:** founder↔investor relationships are exactly this idea, narrowed — and they support real products (Foundersuite, Visible.vc, Affinity) at real prices. Same primitives, one market, money attached. Nobody calls those "personal CRMs"; they're fundraising tools.

So the design question becomes **which vertical**, and the shortlist worth testing:

| Vertical | Why it might work | Why it might not |
|:--|:--|:--|
| **Individual recruiters / 헤드헌터** | The network *is* the job and outlives the employer; enterprise tools (Gem, Beamery) start at $10K+/yr and serve the team, not the person | May just buy the company's tool |
| **Job seekers / 이직 준비자** | Sharp, urgent need; referrals decide outcomes; the update-to-mentors habit fits exactly | Time-boxed — they churn the moment they land |
| **Founders ↔ investors** | Already validated (Foundersuite, Visible) | Already taken; would need a Korea-specific angle |
| **Freelancers / consultants** | Repeat clients are the whole business; direct revenue link makes logging worth it | Blurs back into sales CRM, where incumbents are strong |
| **Mentees maintaining their own advisors** | The gap found in the scan — mentoring platforms all serve the program admin, never the individual | Unclear who pays |

**The combination to test first:** one vertical × Korea (KakaoTalk, 명절, 선배 culture) × the log writing itself. Each alone is a feature; together they're a product a giant can't be bothered to build.

## Challenges
- **Cross-channel capture is the hard problem — and it's mostly walled off.** Email and calendar are accessible. KakaoTalk offers no API for reading personal chats; WhatsApp is Business-API-only; iMessage lives in a local macOS database (hence Clay's full-disk-access ask); LinkedIn DMs are closed; a call leaves nothing but a timestamp. An honest v1 in Korea **cannot auto-ingest the channel that matters most**.
- **Clay/Mesh already exists and is well-built.** Any version of this has to answer "why not Clay?" — and outside of Korea, the answer is thin.
- **Logging friction is the category killer.** Every personal CRM dies here. The walls remove the auto-capture escape hatch for exactly the apps carrying the most conversation — same wall [[Taste Detector]] hit.
- **Privacy** — a file of notes on named people, including what they said in confidence. Clay's full-disk-access requirement is already a stated trust obstacle; local-first is likely non-negotiable.
- **The broadcast tightrope** — an update sent to 30 people has to feel written, not blasted. Get it wrong and it's a newsletter nobody opens; get it right and it may be the whole product.
- **Cadence is fake precision** — a real advisor relationship doesn't run on a 90-day timer, and an "overdue" badge on a mentor feels wrong.

## Open Questions
- **Ingest the channels, or summarize by hand?** The central design fork. *Ingest* gives a real cross-channel thread but is a permissions-and-plumbing product — and in Korea the main channel is closed. *Hand-written* is trivially buildable but re-inherits the friction that kills the category. Likely middle: auto-ingest what's open, make manual entry one line cheap, accept that the thread is a **summary**, not a transcript.
- **Is a summary actually enough?** Possibly better than enough. I don't need messages replayed — I need "last spoke Mar 12 on KakaoTalk, he was hiring for a PM role, I said I'd send my portfolio, I haven't." Four facts beat 300 scrollable messages. If true, the API-wall problem dissolves and this becomes a *writing* product, not a *plumbing* product.
- **Which vertical?** Now the first question, ahead of every feature question — see the shortlist above. Everything else (ingest vs. summary, Korea, broadcast) is downstream of picking one.
- **Is Korea the wedge, or a trap?** It's the clearest open lane (gap #1) and the market I know — but it's small, KakaoTalk-locked, and weak on SaaS willingness-to-pay for personal tools. Does starting there build a moat or a ceiling?
- **Individual or team?** Gem's 46% number says the maintained network is worth real money — but that money sits with hiring *teams* at $10K+/yr, not with individuals. Is the individual recruiter/job-seeker a real buyer, or just the free tier?
- **Can logging be made near-free — or better, self-motivating?** Voice memo → auto-summary is the obvious candidate. But per the framing above, the stronger move is making the *output* the reason to log: the update, the 명절 sweep, the pre-flight brief. Solve that and the category's twenty-year failure mode is solved with it.
- **How does this relate to [[Chronicle - Personal Topic Timeline]]?** Same chronological-archive instinct, aimed at *people* instead of *topics*. Shared engine, or two products that merely rhyme?
- **Does the log connect people?** Two contacts working the same problem, or one who could open a door for another — the vault's [[../research/system/connecting_the_dot|connecting-the-dots]] instinct applied to a professional graph.
